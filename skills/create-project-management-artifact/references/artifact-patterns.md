# Project-management artifact patterns

These are pragmatic authoring patterns, not a claim that one format is proven
best. Preserve the user's organization's conventions when supplied. Use only
the sections that help this reader; avoid filling a framework for its own sake.

## Project brief

Distinguish a proposed initiative from an approved project. State the problem
or opportunity, intended outcome, scope boundaries, known stakeholders and
ownership, success criteria, constraints, and the next approval or action.
Separate agreed scope from open choices. Show unknown funding, acceptance
criteria, or authority as unknown—not as implicitly approved.

Useful visual: an in-scope/out-of-scope comparison or a short outcome-to-work
mapping. An outcome metric needs a definition and measurement basis; do not
invent a baseline or target because the brief has a metric field. Mark suggested
success measures as proposals. Do not manufacture a business case from sparse notes.

## Weekly or project update

Identify the reporting period and source cutoff. Compare prior commitments and
unresolved issues with current evidence before summarizing. Account for material
items as completed, delayed, changed, still open, or unknown. Silence does not
mean completion; say when no previous report was available.

Default to the established 2×2 when no other format is requested:

- **Wins:** source-backed changes toward the goal and their significance.
- **What's coming:** next outcomes/actions, known owners, dates, dependencies;
  distinguish commitments from suggestions and estimates.
- **Current blockers:** problems already happening, impact, and help needed.
- **Risks ahead:** possible events, consequences, warning triggers, and known
  responses. Mark suggested mitigations as proposals and retain important
  longer-horizon risks.

Lead with overall position and any decision/help request. Add a compact
commitment check and optional supporting sources. Do not impose four visual
cards on a short email or make up wins during a quiet week. A supported learning
or proposed adjustment can close the loop; a summary is not a full retrospective.

## Milestone and dependency plan

Distinguish baseline commitments, current forecasts, targets, and undated work.
For each material milestone, show the outcome, completion/acceptance criterion,
known accountable owner, supported timing, and necessary prerequisites. A date
change should preserve the old and new dates and known reason rather than erase
the earlier commitment. If the baseline is unavailable, do not claim a slip.

Use a timeline only when dates support one, otherwise a dependency sequence or
milestone table. Label arrows by the known dependency; do not invent a relationship
from source order alone. Flag contradictory ordering, missing acceptance gates,
or a target that falls before its necessary prerequisite. Without enough data,
show the scheduling question rather than compute a fictitious finish date,
critical path, capacity forecast, or completion percentage.

## Risk, issue, and dependency brief

Keep distinct: a risk is a possible event, an issue is already occurring, and
a dependency is a needed input or preceding outcome. A dependency may be healthy;
it is not automatically a blocker. Use stable source IDs when supplied.

For material entries show objective affected, event or current condition,
consequence, known owner, next action, and trigger/checkpoint when available.
For dependencies identify the supplying party and receiving work only when
known. Represent response plans separately from proposed mitigations. Do not
invent probabilities, severity scores, or rank people by number of blockers.
If a requested scoring scheme lacks inputs, display unassessed values and ask
for the decision-relevant inputs. Put urgent escalation and its actual record
separately from routine reporting; an artifact does not prove anyone was notified.

## Decision brief or record

First distinguish a pending choice from a decision actually made. For a pending
choice, show the decision, known authority and timing, viable supplied options,
criteria, evidence, tradeoffs, and a recommendation only when supportable.
Mark newly suggested options as proposals and include the status quo when it is
a meaningful alternative. Do not invent a winner to satisfy the brief structure.

For a record, preserve the actual decision, date, decision-maker, rationale,
important alternatives, consequences, and agreed follow-up. Keep proposals,
unresolved dissent, and open questions separate from the recorded outcome.
Meeting discussion and absent objections are not evidence of approval. Link
to the source of approval when available and audience-safe. A generated record
does not itself approve the decision or assign the follow-up work.
