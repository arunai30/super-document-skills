# Product decision deck

Read [the shared base](_common.md) first; this recipe adds to it.

## Must

- One self-contained static HTML file, built on the starter's `main.share-presentation` and `section.share-slide` structure. No scripts, navigation buttons or slide controls.
- Every slide fits its 16:9 frame at 1280px wide. If a slide's content doesn't fit, cut words or split the slide; never let it grow taller.
- About 40 words of body text per slide; slide titles about 8 to 10 words.
- Let the content set the length. Put everything the decision depends on in the deck; when a point needs more than about 40 words, continue it on another slide rather than cutting it. A quick or short deck makes its main case in 3 to 5 slides, with any continuation slides after them. If the user names a slide count, keep to it and say in your reply what didn't fit.
- Give every option, including waiting or doing nothing, the same numbers (cost, time, effect) where the material allows, and add up the totals the decision turns on, such as total time to a result or total cost.
- Use only facts and numbers from the user's material.
- Sort every gap in the notes. A choice only the reader can make (approve spend, name an owner, set a date) goes on the slide as a full-sentence question with its reason. A fact the requester knows (a date, a figure, who is involved) goes to the requester as a question in your reply, never to the reader; if a slide needs it to stay concrete, state it as a plain assumption instead. Never leave "not set", "still missing" or "TBD" on a slide.
- Before handing over, render the deck with `check.mjs`, look at every slide, fix what is wrong and check again (see "Check it, fix it, check again").

Create a decision deck that helps a busy executive make or approve one product choice. It must work as a pre-read, without you presenting. Build the argument, not a template: there is no fixed slide order. Slide 1 answers first: the decision needed, your recommendation and the ask. Every slide title is a claim, so the titles read in order make the whole case; keep one idea per slide. Before the options, frame the problem, who it affects and the decision criteria in priority order; if the notes don't rank them, suggest an order and give the reason in a sentence on the slide; don't tag labels or headers as "proposed". Compare two or three real options on the same criteria and units, including waiting or doing nothing when that is credible. No straw options. Show, don't tell: every slide whose claim rests on numbers, time or structure carries a chart or diagram (for example a capacity timeline, a to-scale bar, a before/after or a simple flow), with its takeaway in the title. Prefer a picture to a table and a table to prose; the options comparison can be a table. Draw charts and diagrams as inline SVG with a viewBox, so labels and boxes scale together and nothing spills out; avoid building diagrams from positioned HTML boxes. Wrap each chart in a .chart container, as the starter does, so on phones it scrolls sideways instead of shrinking its labels. Own the cost in a few short lines: what the recommendation gives up, the strongest argument against it and what happens to the option that loses. Say in a line each how hard the choice is to reverse, the main risks with mitigations, and what evidence would change the recommendation. When the evidence is inconclusive, a scoped test can be the recommendation. If the user says they don't know what to recommend, don't manufacture conviction: set out the options, what each depends on and what would settle it, and give a lean only where the evidence supports one, saying it is a lean. End with a specific ask, and make the closing slide's title the ask itself, with no second large line restating it: what decision, from whom, by when, and what it does and does not commit (direction, spend, staffing). A deadline that follows from the notes, such as a lead time or renewal date, is fine if you say what it follows from. Let the content set the length: every option, date, total and risk the decision depends on gets its place, and a slide that would pass about 40 words continues on the next slide instead of being cut. A quick or short deck makes its main case in 3 to 5 slides, with any continuation slides after; when the user names a slide count, keep to it and list in your reply what didn't fit. Keep each title to one claim of about 8 to 10 words (under about 60 characters) so it usually fits on one line and never more than two; do not narrow titles with a width limit; the cover title is larger, so keep it to about 40 characters, with any qualifier in a short subtitle; keep the rest of a slide to about 40 words; say nothing twice; move supporting detail to appendix slides at the end. Stay faithful: use only facts and numbers from the user's material and check every calculation. A missing measurement is not an observed difference. Never invent approval, owners, dates, effort or targets, and never add a commitment, metric or promise the user didn't make. Don't overstate: "customers who asked for it", not "customers who will stay because of it". When the decision needs a choice only the reader can make, such as a date, an owner or a budget, ask for it as part of the ask in a full sentence with the reason (for example "Who should own the rollout? It needs one person accountable."). Never write it as a fragment like "Please confirm: owner" or "Confirm dates". When a fact is missing that the requester would know, such as a date, a figure or who is involved, don't put it to the reader. If a slide needs it to stay concrete, state a plain assumption in a full sentence (for example "Assuming the pilot starts in March, results arrive by June."); otherwise write the slide without it. Either way, list it in your reply to the requester as a question to answer before sending. A statement that something still needs work ("needs validation", "needs engineering rates") is a working note: either ask the reader a real question or leave it out. Never label gaps "still missing", "not set", "open", "TBD", "unspecified" or "to confirm". While working, keep track of which numbers are observed, calculated or estimated. On the slides, flag uncertainty in plain words only where it could change the decision, and, if the user named their sources, list them on the closing slide as they named them; never make up source names. Do not tag every claim. Write like a direct colleague: plain verbs and specific nouns, no marketing tone or hype words, no stacked adjectives, and no lists padded to three items. Keep it clean but never bare. Lay each slide out as described in the shared base and "Layout for this deck" below, using the full width: a chart, a lead line beside the evidence (.split), two columns (.cols), a short sequence (.steps), a label-and-statement grid (.facts, at most 4 rows of about one line each) for cost, risks, reversibility or open items, where each label is plain words ("What we give up") and each row is a full sentence a VP can read cold, never shorthand like "Cost / countercase" or a "(proposed)" tag, or one short statement set large (.big). A slide that is only a title and a few lines of plain text looks unfinished; give a light slide one large statement or a bigger chart rather than spreading it out. Use large type, one accent colour, charts drawn to scale with labels on the marks instead of legends, and balanced titles with no single-word last line. Avoid the AI-default look: rows of identical cards or big-number tiles, icons, gradients, emoji, decorative images and centred text. Nothing meant for you, such as scale notes or working labels, appears on a slide. The reader has not seen your notes: never mention "the notes" or "supplied" material, never address the deck to "my manager", and name a source only as the user named it. Size type and spacing relative to the slide, as the starter does with container units, so every slide keeps its proportions at any screen width and no text is ever clipped. Keep the starter's look (paper slides, a light cover with an accent band, one blue accent, the accent rule above each title, the footer line with slide numbers) unless the user gives a brand, style or template; then change the colour and font variables, and follow their template's frame if they gave one. In charts, use .mark for the recommended option, .mark-other for the rest and .label or .label-on for text; in the options table, tint the recommended column with class="pick". If the decision or the options are missing, ask; otherwise proceed and keep unknowns visible in the ask. This is not a sales pitch or a status update. Show the draft for review; publishing needs separate approval.

## Layout for this deck

Every slide has a fixed frame: the padding, the footer line with the slide number and, on every slide after the cover, the title at the top in the same place, so a reader flipping through finds each claim where they expect it. Everything under the title goes in one `.stage`, which places it as one block a little above the middle of the space between the title and the footer, so a slide with less on it still looks balanced and stays close to its title. Add `stage-top` to a dense slide, such as a full table or a long list, that reads better starting right under the title. Never stretch or space content out to fill the slide. On the cover, the stage holds everything.

This layout is the default; if the user asks for a different one or gives a template, follow theirs.

## Before you hand it over

- Does `main` carry one style class, and is the closing slide's title the ask with no second headline under it?

- Reading only slide 1, does an executive know the decision, the recommendation and the ask?
- Do the slide titles, each at most two lines, read in order to make the full argument with no repeats?
- Is each title at the top of its slide and the body under it in one `.stage`, sitting a little above the middle of the space below it (or starting right under the title with `stage-top` when dense), and does every slide after the cover use its width, with no slide that is a few lines of text in empty space?
- Is everything the decision depends on in the deck (each option, and the dates and totals it turns on), split across continuation slides rather than cut, with any slide count the user named kept?
- Is each chart or diagram an SVG drawn to scale, with every label inside the chart and clear of other marks (beside a bar when the bar is too short to hold it), and no slide over about 40 words of body text?
- Are the options compared on the same ranked criteria, with the recommendation's cost, strongest counterargument and reversibility stated briefly?
- Is every choice the reader must make asked as a full-sentence question with a reason, is every fact only the requester knows asked in your reply (and, where a slide needs it, shown as a stated assumption rather than a question to the reader), and is there no "not set", "still missing", "TBD", mention of "the notes", shorthand label, "(proposed)" tag, made-up source name, hype or marketing tone?
- Does every number match the notes, with uncertainty flagged only where it matters and no invented owners, dates or approvals?
- Did `check.mjs` report no problems (or only ones you looked at and accepted), and did you look at every screenshot? If you could not render, say so.

## Optional share/artifacts publishing

Publishing is optional and needs the user's separate approval. With a connected share/artifacts client, publish an approved deck with `publish_presentation` and choose who can see it as described in [the publishing notes](../references/share-artifacts.md). Build the deck from this recipe, not from the `presentation-deck` recipe those notes mention. The share/artifacts viewer keeps this recipe's static inline SVG charts (shapes, paths, text, gradients, clip paths and markers).
